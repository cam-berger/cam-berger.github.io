04 · Multimodal ML

Multimodal chest X-ray

Can clinical context make a chest X-ray classifier better? A pretrained image encoder fused with text written before the X-ray, across twelve MIMIC-CXR findings.

Years
2025–2026 · Independent research
My role
Sole researcher and engineer
Status
Third version complete, October 2026.

The problem

A radiologist never reads a chest X-ray alone. They know why the patient came in, their vital signs, and their labs, and that context changes what they look for. Most models see only the image, and labeling images well enough to train them is slow and expensive.

What I built

A pipeline that reads each chest X-ray in MIMIC-CXR along with what was written before it: why the exam was ordered, the ER chief complaint, and the patient’s last report. It starts from RAD-DINO, an image model already trained on 883,000 chest X-rays.

How it works

  1. 1

    Select

    One front-view image per study, and only text from before the scan.

  2. 2

    Train

    Fine-tune RAD-DINO for three epochs, about 50 minutes on one GPU.

  3. 3

    Score

    Make every choice on a validation split, then score each test set once.

Why it matters

This is my first attempt at the hard part of learning from many modalities at once, including clinical ones: fusing an image with the record around it, and proving the record helps. Here the record helps a little, every time, and measuring that honestly took more work than building the model.

Tools

Python, PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, scikit-learn, pandas. Models: RAD-DINO, MedSigLIP, DINOv2 and DenseNet-121; TF-IDF, BERT, BiomedBERT and ClinicalBERT. Data: MIMIC-CXR-JPG 2.1.0 and its reports, MIMIC-IV-ED 2.2, MIMIC-IV 3.1, and the CheXpert test set. v3 also tried TypeSafe’s Jev. Compute: a laptop, plus about 16 hours of rented A100, GH200 and H100 time.

Results

The standard benchmark

0.815

with the reason for the exam. The best published model with the same inputs gets 0.794.

From the image alone it scores 0.807 on MIMIC-CXR’s official test split. Adding everything written before the X-ray brings it to 0.825.

What the text adds

+0.016

from all the text written before the X-ray

Most of it comes from the reason for the exam and the last report. Vital signs, age, and sex add nothing.

Graded by a radiologist

+0.029

when a radiologist’s labels replace the automatic ones, on the same 596 studies

The biggest jumps are pneumonia and an enlarged heart, where the automatic labeler flags more cases than the radiologist does. The model was better than its labels made it look.

Another hospital

0.904

on five findings at Stanford, without training on a single CheXpert label

That puts it level with CheXzero and KAD, two published models trained on MIMIC-CXR, and almost all of the lead over CheXzero is atelectasis. Retraining with a new seed moves every score by 0.002 at most.

From v1 to v3

How the number moved

0.701 → 0.823

on v1’s own test split

v1 built everything from scratch. For v2 I kept the question and the twelve findings and rebuilt the rest.

v3 · A bigger model

0 of 3

jobs where TypeSafe’s Jev beat v2’s simple tools

For v3 I tested whether a large hosted model could do better. Jev took three jobs: final classifier, report labeler and source of training labels. It lost or tied every time. Its one big win, on Stanford’s test set, came from how two findings are labeled there, and a one-line rule gets most of it back.

What changed in v2

Measurement
v1 picked its best epoch on its own test set. v2 scores each test set once.
Images
31% of v1’s images were side views. v2 uses one frontal image per study.
Studies
A time-parsing bug dropped most morning studies. v2 uses all 218,000 frontal ones.
Text
v1’s text held data from after the X-ray. v2 uses only what came before.
Fusion
v1’s cross-attention was really just a linear layer. v2 simply adds the text at the end.

Limits

The labels come from an automatic labeler that undersells every model by about 0.03. Only front views are used.

What’s next

Read Stanford’s clinical histories alongside the image, try higher resolution, and test at a hospital RAD-DINO has never seen.

v2 · How it reads an X-ray

Each of the twelve findings learns which patches of the image to focus on, and the text written before the X-ray nudges the score.

What each part does

Pretraining on chest X-rays: +0.060. Letting each finding pick its patches: +0.015. Fine-tuning: +0.010. The text: about +0.02.

What it doesn’t see

Side views, detail finer than 518 pixels, or anything written after the X-ray.

v1 · Anomaly detection

Trained only on normal chests, the model rebuilds every X-ray as if it were normal. Where the rebuild fails is where to look.

What it found

Across 4,922 abnormal studies, it had the most trouble rebuilding pleural changes, collapsed lungs, pneumonia and fractures. Oddly, consolidation was the easiest (Fig. 10).

What it didn’t show

These averages only compare sick chests to each other. I never tested whether the error actually separates normal from abnormal. In v2 I dropped anomaly scores, because they can’t tell you which finding is there.
